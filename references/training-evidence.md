# Cycling training evidence snapshot

Last reviewed: 2026-09-20. Refresh claims that materially affect a new plan when newer evidence is likely.

## Evidence hierarchy

Use systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus statements, and controlled studies for prescriptions. Coach surveys and elite cases show current practice. YouTube, Bilibili, Reddit, X, podcasts, and news reveal questions, language, adherence problems, and emerging practices; they do not establish efficacy.

### Successful cases are comparison anchors, not prescriptions

Create the athlete-only plan before inspecting a successful case, then compare it using [case-comparison.md](case-comparison.md). Individual responses to the same standardized endurance program vary because of baseline phenotype, dose normalization, recovery, sleep, life stress, nutrition, and measurement error. A 2023 cycling study also found that normalizing interval intensity to an individually sustainable session improved completion compared with a fixed percentage of incremental-test maximum.

Practical use: prefer the athlete's own successful history, require comparable goals and tests, scale case dose to recent tolerance, and show adopted and rejected deltas. Do not treat a professional success story or a group mean as the expected result.

Sources: [individual variation review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24807838/), [cycling intensity-normalization study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37966510/).

## Current findings and boundaries

### Durability is a distinct performance dimension

A 2025 review describes durability as the resilience of physiological variables during prolonged exercise and notes that protocols are not yet standardized. A 2025 home-based study in 20 trained amateur cyclists found roughly 10% mean loss in 5- and 20-minute power after 1000 kJ, with no significant relationship to VO2max or relative FTP. The sample is small, so use late-ride fade as a useful profile rather than a universal score.

Practical use: assess long-ride power/HR drift, RPE drift, and the ability to complete controlled work after accumulated work. Standardize route, duration, fueling, and environment before comparing tests.

Sources: [Experimental Physiology methodological review](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/bitstreams/93b3d2fd-6bea-484f-b9a6-22da90845a4f/download), [BMC amateur-cyclist study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-025-01238-8).

### No intensity-distribution model wins for everyone

A 2025 individual-participant network meta-analysis covering 13 studies and 348 athletes found no overall difference between polarized and pyramidal training for VO2max or time-trial performance. Subgroup results suggested competitive athletes may respond better to polarized work and recreational athletes to pyramidal work. A survey of 117 cycling coaches found 94% changed distribution by season phase; moving from pyramidal toward polarized training near racing was the most common strategy.

Practical use: preserve a large low-intensity base, then choose moderate and high-intensity doses by training age, available volume, phase, and event demands. Do not prescribe literal 80/20 by default.

Sources: [2025 network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/), [cycling coach survey](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17479541251362199).

### Heavy strength training can improve cycling performance

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies and 262 cyclists found heavy strength training improved cycling efficiency, anaerobic power, and cycling performance, without significant changes in VO2max or maximal metabolic steady state. Certainty was low, so the best program is not settled. A 2025 survey of 147 professional road cyclists found two weekly sessions were common in the off/pre-season and one maintenance session in the race season.

Practical use: when appropriate, use a preparation phase to build maximal strength and reduce to a low-volume maintenance dose in season. Protect recovery around key rides.

Sources: [heavy-strength meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/), [professional practice study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0328195).

### Fueling supports late-ride power, but high rates are individual

A 2026 crossover trial in 16 trained cyclists/triathletes found 120 g/h carbohydrate attenuated the decline in critical power after three hours more than 60 g/h or water. It did not prevent fatigue and did not preserve W'. Elite cases commonly report 60–120 g/h chosen by stage demands, but this practice occurs with gut training and professional nutrition support.

Practical use: fuel key and long sessions, progress intake from known tolerance, rehearse the event plan, and record GI response. Do not copy 120–180 g/h professional anecdotes into ordinary training.

Sources: [2026 graded-carbohydrate trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42322010/), [2025 WorldTour fueling case](https://www.cyclingnews.com/features/mango-flavour-and-120g-per-hour-how-does-tadej-pogacar-fuel-in-a-tour-de-france-stage/).

### VO2 session format should serve accumulated oxygen demand and adherence

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis found work intervals of at least two minutes produced more time near VO2max than shorter formats in acute studies, and accumulated HIIT duration was positively related to time at high oxygen uptake. Acute time near VO2max is a proxy rather than proof of superior long-term adaptation.

Practical use: favor repeatable 2–5 minute work intervals when the goal is aerobic power, but use short/variable formats when they improve execution, event specificity, or motivation.

Source: [2026 HIIT systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42237396/).

### Monitor several signals, not one readiness number

Recent monitoring reviews emphasize that no single marker captures the interaction among fitness, fatigue, recovery, illness, and life stress. HRV is sensitive to measurement conditions and non-training factors.

Practical use: combine completion quality, RPE, sleep, soreness, motivation, illness, resting HR/HRV trend, and objective performance.

Sources: [2026 HRV-guided training review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2026.1858271/full), [multidimensional monitoring framework](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13388359/).

### Heat preparation and female-athlete planning need individualization

Heat acclimation is appropriate when the target event is hot; consensus guidance uses repeated exercise-heat exposure over roughly one to two weeks. It adds physiological strain and is not a generic substitute for normal training.

High-quality evidence does not support universal menstrual-phase training periodization. Track reproducible individual symptoms and adapt sessions, recovery, and fueling when they matter.

Sources: [heat consensus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4602249/), [2025 elite-athlete consensus](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.70112).

### Low-cadence training remains an optional specificity tool

A systematic review found no strong evidence that imposed low-cadence training improves performance more than freely chosen cadence.

Practical use: prescribe it for terrain/cadence specificity or controlled torque practice, not as a proven universal shortcut.

Source: [low-cadence systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28095074/).

### Power-to-weight goals require energy-availability safeguards

Power-to-weight is a ratio, so the same target can be pursued through more power, less mass, or both. Cycling's high energy cost and emphasis on low mass create a risk of problematic low energy availability. Current body-composition guidance emphasizes athlete health, confidential handling of body-composition data, infrequent purposeful assessment, and multidisciplinary support rather than chronic weight pressure.

Practical use: define the target duration and track absolute watts alongside W/kg. Default to power development unless the nutrition and health intake supports a time-limited, modest body-composition phase. A coach skill should flag possible risk rather than diagnose RED-S.

Sources: [UCI Sports Nutrition Project on energy availability in cycling](https://assets.ctfassets.net/761l7gh5x5an/6XYj9tVoBM6Rj2uLG44ZNM/8c254527860b29b75868165abde1f516/UCI_Burke_2026_Energy_availability_in_Cycling.pdf), [IOC body-composition best practice](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752006/), [IOC RED-S consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994).

### Model parameters need valid maximal efforts

FTP/CP, P-max, and W′/FRC describe different parts of the power-duration relationship. W′ size, W′ reconstitution, peak sprint, and repeated-sprint ability are related but are not interchangeable. Intervals.icu estimates are only as useful as the maximal efforts and recording quality in the selected period.

Practical use: inspect the power curve and effort coverage before building a block around a model value. Use a repeatable duration-specific test. For sprint goals, track absolute watts, W/kg, cadence, and whether the effort is fresh or fatigue-loaded. For durability tests, standardize the intensity as well as accumulated work; a 2025 review found higher-intensity prior work impaired performance with less accumulated work.

Source: [2025 durability workload-intensity review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-025-05885-0).

### Supplements are optional and goal-specific

The Australian Institute of Sport classifies sports foods and supplements by evidence, safety, and permitted status. Even its Group A products are supported only for specific situations and evidence-based protocols; medical supplements such as iron or vitamin D require appropriate assessment and expert oversight.

Practical use: record the exact product, dose, timing, purpose, response, medication interactions, and batch testing. Do not let supplements substitute for adequate food, energy availability, sleep, or a suitable training stimulus.

Source: [AIS Sports Supplement Framework](https://www.ais.gov.au/nutrition/supplements).
